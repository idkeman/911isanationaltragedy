Dinosaur extinction

The end-Cretaceous mass extinction around 66 million years ago, the Chicxulub impact, environmental disruption, and the survival of birds.

03 / Deep time

The end of the dinosaurs: a world-changing mass extinction.

About 66 million years ago, Earth experienced one of the most consequential biological crises in its history. A large asteroid struck near what is now the Yucatán Peninsula in Mexico. The impact formed the Chicxulub crater and triggered environmental changes that drove the extinction of non-avian dinosaurs and many other groups of organisms. This was not simply the end of a few famous animals: it was a global ecological upheaval that reshaped life on land and in the oceans.

Important distinction: Non-avian dinosaurs went extinct, but birds survived. Birds are the surviving dinosaur lineage. The event was not an instant disappearance of every dinosaur, nor did every kind of life die.

What was Earth like before the impact?

During the Late Cretaceous, dinosaurs occupied a wide range of ecological roles on land. Tyrannosaurs and other large predators hunted or scavenged; hadrosaurs and ceratopsians were among the major herbivores in some regions; armored ankylosaurs and many smaller dinosaurs occupied other niches. Flowering plants had diversified, continents were arranged differently from today, and high sea levels created broad shallow seas in some regions. Marine ecosystems included ammonites, large marine reptiles, fish, plankton, and many other organisms.

Dinosaurs were not one uniform group. They included animals with very different sizes, diets, habitats, and life histories. Nor were they the only organisms affected. The K–Pg event also eliminated pterosaurs, ammonites, many marine reptiles, and numerous plankton and other species. The impact disrupted food webs across land and sea.

Life on land

Non-avian dinosaurs included large herbivores, predators, and smaller species. Mammals were also present, but most were relatively small compared with the largest dinosaurs and occupied different ecological roles.

Life in the oceans

Ammonites, mosasaurs, plesiosaurs, and many plankton groups were part of complex marine ecosystems. The extinction changed ocean food webs as well as terrestrial ones.

A changing environment

Sea levels, climate, habitats, and volcanic activity were changing during the Late Cretaceous. These background conditions matter when evaluating how ecosystems responded to a sudden global catastrophe.

Not all dinosaurs were the same

Birds are living dinosaurs. The extinction eliminated all known non-avian dinosaur groups, while a limited number of avian lineages survived and later diversified into the birds alive today.

The asteroid and the Chicxulub crater

The impact site lies beneath the Yucatán Peninsula and the adjacent Gulf of Mexico. The buried Chicxulub structure is roughly 180 kilometers across. Geological evidence links it to the K–Pg boundary: a thin layer found around the world contains unusually high concentrations of iridium and other impact-related material. Researchers have also identified shocked quartz, impact glass and spherules, and a crater whose age matches the extinction boundary. Together, these independent lines of evidence make the impact explanation exceptionally strong.

The impactor is commonly described as an asteroid roughly 10 kilometers across, although estimates vary. It struck at enormous speed and released an extraordinary amount of energy. The collision excavated rock, generated intense heat and pressure, and sent material high into the atmosphere. The precise local sequence depended on the geology at the impact site, including carbonate and sulfur-rich rocks that contributed material capable of affecting the atmosphere.

Why scientists are confident: The case does not rest on a single fossil or one dramatic crater. It combines a globally recognizable boundary layer, impact minerals and ejecta, a matching crater, precise dating, and a sudden pattern of extinction in the fossil record.

How could one impact cause a global extinction?

The immediate devastation near the impact site would have been extreme, but the global extinction mechanism involved the atmosphere and food webs. Dust, soot, and sulfur-bearing aerosols thrown into the atmosphere reduced the sunlight reaching Earth's surface. The resulting darkness and cooling disrupted photosynthesis on land and in the oceans. Wildfires, acid rain, severe temperature swings, and other effects likely contributed in different places and at different times.

When sunlight was reduced, plants and photosynthetic plankton—the base of many food webs—were hit hard. Herbivores lost food, and predators then lost prey. Marine food chains were also disrupted. The severity was not identical everywhere: organisms with different diets, habitats, body sizes, reproductive strategies, or access to shelter and food had different chances of surviving the crisis.

Impact winter

Fine particles and aerosols in the atmosphere reduced sunlight and cooled the surface. The duration and intensity varied by model and by the type of material injected into the atmosphere.

Food-web collapse

Reduced photosynthesis undermined food supplies. The loss of plants and plankton cascaded through herbivores, predators, and marine consumers.

Heat, fires, and tsunamis

The impact generated intense local heating and seismic effects, while the ocean impact produced enormous waves. Evidence for fires and other short-term effects is found in boundary deposits, although their regional intensity varied.

Uneven survival

Some small animals, freshwater organisms, burrowing species, and animals able to use varied food sources survived. Survival was not simply a matter of being strong or weak; ecological circumstances mattered.

A timeline of the extinction

The event happened over geological time, not within a human-style historical timeline. The dates below describe the sequence as reconstructed from rocks, fossils, and impact evidence.

Chicxulub impact

HOURS TO DAYS AFTER IMPACT

Immediate regional destruction

Near the impact zone, extreme heat, ejecta, seismic effects, and ocean disturbances devastate environments. Material from the impact spreads far beyond the crater.

FOLLOWING MONTHS TO YEARS

Global environmental disruption

Atmospheric dust and aerosols reduce sunlight and disrupt climate and photosynthesis. Food webs on land and in the oceans experience severe stress and collapse in many places.

Mass extinction recorded in rocks

Non-avian dinosaurs and many other groups disappear from the fossil record at the boundary between the Cretaceous and Paleogene periods.

EARLY PALEOGENE

Survivors and recovery

Surviving organisms repopulate disrupted ecosystems. Mammals diversify over subsequent millions of years, while surviving bird lineages also expand into new ecological roles.

Was the asteroid the only factor?

The Chicxulub impact is widely accepted as the primary trigger of the abrupt K–Pg extinction. However, it occurred during a period of major volcanic activity in India, especially the Deccan Traps eruptions. Those eruptions released large quantities of gases over an extended interval and contributed to climate and environmental change. Researchers continue to study how much volcanism affected ecosystems before and after the impact, and whether it made some communities more vulnerable.

The fossil record is incomplete and uneven. It is difficult to determine whether a particular species disappeared just before or just after the boundary when the relevant rock layers are separated by thousands or tens of thousands of years. Some studies identify longer-term changes in particular dinosaur groups or regions; other analyses find little evidence for a global collapse in dinosaur diversity immediately before the impact. The strongest overall evidence points to an abrupt extinction event associated with Chicxulub, against a background of environmental change.

A careful conclusion: “Asteroid versus volcano” is too simple. The impact best explains the sudden global extinction pattern. Deccan volcanism and longer-term ecological changes may have influenced the conditions in which the impact occurred and the recovery that followed. The relative contribution of those background factors remains an active research topic.

Why did some animals survive?

Survival depended on ecological position and biological traits, not on a simple ranking of which animals were toughest. Small body size could mean lower food requirements. Burrowing or aquatic habitats may have provided some protection from immediate surface conditions. Flexible diets, seeds, detritus, insects, and other food sources may have helped certain lineages endure when photosynthesis-based food webs were disrupted. These are broad patterns, not universal rules: many small animals also went extinct.

Birds survived, but that does not mean modern birds are unchanged Cretaceous survivors. The surviving avian lineages were a small fraction of the diversity that existed before the extinction. Their descendants diversified over millions of years. Crocodilians, turtles, amphibians, fish, mammals, and many other groups also survived, though their communities were altered.

What changed after the extinction?

Mammals diversified

The extinction removed many dominant terrestrial animals and opened ecological opportunities. Mammals diversified substantially during the Paleogene, eventually producing a wide range of body sizes and ecological roles.

Birds continued the dinosaur story

Birds are the surviving branch of theropod dinosaurs. The extinction removed non-avian dinosaurs while birds continued and diversified into new ecological roles.

Marine ecosystems reorganized

Ammonites and several large marine reptile groups disappeared. Plankton communities changed, and marine food webs recovered and reorganized over time.

Recovery took time

Life did not instantly return to its previous state. Ecosystems rebuilt over thousands to millions of years, with new communities and evolutionary opportunities emerging gradually.

Common claims and what the evidence says

“The asteroid killed every dinosaur.”

Not exactly. All known non-avian dinosaurs disappeared, but birds survived. Birds are dinosaurs in the evolutionary and scientific classification sense: they descend from theropod dinosaurs. The accurate statement is that the K–Pg extinction eliminated non-avian dinosaurs while a small number of avian lineages survived.

“The dinosaurs all died on the same day.”

The impact happened at a particular moment, but the extinction unfolded through a chain of environmental disruptions and food-web effects. Some organisms near the impact zone died immediately; many populations elsewhere were affected over subsequent months, years, and longer intervals. The fossil record captures a geological boundary, not a single synchronized moment for every species.

“The asteroid theory is just a guess.”

The impact explanation is supported by multiple independent forms of evidence: the Chicxulub crater, the global iridium-rich boundary layer, shocked minerals, impact ejecta, precise dating, and the pattern of extinction. Scientific debate focuses more on details of the environmental effects and the role of other factors than on whether a major impact occurred.

“Volcanoes, not the asteroid, caused the extinction.”

Deccan Traps volcanism was substantial and likely affected climate and ecosystems. But the close timing of Chicxulub with the abrupt extinction boundary, plus the global impact evidence and extinction pattern, make the asteroid the best-supported primary trigger. Volcanism may have contributed to background stress and recovery conditions.

“Dinosaurs were already dying out, so the impact barely mattered.”

Some studies find declines or ecological restructuring in certain dinosaur groups and regions before the boundary. Other research finds little evidence for a global, long-term decline and shows major dinosaur groups persisting close to the impact. The fossil record is incomplete, but the abrupt disappearance at the K–Pg boundary strongly supports a major external shock.

“Humans could have lived alongside non-avian dinosaurs.”

There is no credible fossil evidence that humans and non-avian dinosaurs coexisted. Non-avian dinosaurs disappeared about 66 million years ago. The earliest known members of the human lineage appeared vastly later, and modern humans are separated from the extinction by tens of millions of years.

“The impact made Earth completely lifeless.”

No. The extinction was devastating, but many organisms survived. The oceans, freshwater environments, and land ecosystems retained lineages that later repopulated and diversified. The event changed the course of evolution; it did not sterilize the planet.

“The extinction was bad only for dinosaurs.”

The K–Pg event was a mass extinction affecting many groups, including ammonites, pterosaurs, marine reptiles, and numerous microscopic organisms. Its consequences reached across terrestrial and marine food webs. Dinosaurs are the best-known victims, not the only ones.

The central point: The end of the non-avian dinosaurs was a real, global mass extinction, not a simple story of one rock hitting Earth and every animal instantly vanishing. The impact's environmental aftermath, the ecosystems it disrupted, the organisms that survived, and the role of volcanic activity together explain why this event changed the history of life.
